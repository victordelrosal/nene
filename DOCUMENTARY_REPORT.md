# IRENE (YRENE) VIZCAÍNO PELAYO
## A Life Across a Century: Documentary Research Report

**Subject:** Irene (Yrene) Vizcaíno Pelayo
**Born:** May 24, 1924, Tonaya, Jalisco, Mexico
**Died:** April 14, 2019, Villa de Álvarez, Colima, Mexico
**Age at death:** 94 years, 10 months, 20 days

**Report compiled from:** Civil marriage certificate (1937), church marriage certificate (1937), death certificate (2019), two handwritten family trees, and a genealogical narrative authored by Roberto Ahumada Vizcaíno (June 26, 2020), supplemented by historical and geographical research.

---

## PART I: THE WOMAN

### 1.1 Identity and Names

Her birth name was **Irene Vizcaíno Pelayo**, though across all documents, her first name alternates between "Irene" and "Yrene," an older, rural spelling common in the ranchos of southern Jalisco. On her civil marriage certificate (1937) she appears as "IRENE VIZCAINO." On her church marriage certificate, she is "Yrene Vizcaíno." On her death certificate (2019), she is "YRENE VIZCAINO PELAYO." Both spellings were used interchangeably throughout her life.

After marriage, she was known as **Irene Vizcaíno de Ahumada**. The family referred to her as **Nene**.

### 1.2 Birth

- **Date:** May 24, 1924
- **Place:** Tonaya, municipality of Tonaya, Jalisco, Mexico
- **Specific origin:** Rancho de Los González, a small rural settlement within the municipality of Tonaya, in the Sierra de Amula region of southern Jalisco
- **Father:** Faustino Vizcaíno Pelayo, Mexican, campesino (farmer)
- **Mother:** Crescenciana Pelayo Naranjo, Mexican, campesina

The marriage certificate (1937) describes Irene as "soltera, mexicana, dedicada a los quehaceres de casa" (single, Mexican, dedicated to household duties), and states she was 13 years old, "originaria del Rancho de Los González, Jal., y vecina de Villa de Álvarez, Col." This tells us that at some point before May 1937, the family had already migrated from Tonaya, Jalisco, to Villa de Álvarez, Colima.

**The mystery of her birthday:** Irene's mother told her she had been born on a day of the Virgin, and Irene always celebrated her birthday on **May 13**, the feast day of the Virgen de Fátima. The family honored this tradition for nearly 95 years, up to her last birthday. It was only when the family obtained a passport for Irene to travel to Europe that they discovered her official registration date was **May 24**. It did not matter: for Irene, her day was always the 13th.

### 1.3 Physical Description and Character

The genealogical narrative, written by Roberto Ahumada Vizcaíno, describes Irene as a **"cabaladora"** (a woman who labored, possibly from "cabal," meaning capable/complete; or a regional term for a hardworking woman). She is described as "hija de campesinos, trabajadores de ese rancho" (daughter of campesinos, workers of that ranch). The narrative also notes the "condición de estas personas, que son sobrias, lean ni escribir" (the condition of these people, who are sober/austere, [who could] neither read nor write).

**Irene was illiterate.** She could not read or write. This was not unusual for a girl born in 1924 in a remote rancho of the Sierra de Amula, especially during the Cristero War years when schools were closed, teachers were persecuted, and families were displaced. Despite this, she raised 18 children and lived to be nearly 95 years old.

**Key observations about the marriage:**

1. **Irene was 13 years old.** The legal minimum marriage age for girls in 1930s Mexico was 14. The marriage required special dispensations, including parental consent supplied through a representative (Francisco de Sales Vizcaíno) and a Church dispensation "de edad por la pretensa" (for age, by reason of the bride).

2. **Her mother was already dead.** The civil certificate describes Crescenciana Pelayo as "finada" (deceased). Irene was a motherless child at the time of her marriage, just 13 years old.

3. **Francisco de Sales Vizcaíno** played a double role: he represented the bride's father in the civil ceremony AND signed the church marriage certificate as a priest ("Pbro." = Presbítero). He was both a family member and a clergyman, a Vizcaíno who had entered the priesthood. The family tree confirms "Francisco de Sales (sacerdote)" as one of the children of an earlier Vizcaíno generation.

4. **The marriage took place in Comala**, not in the couple's town of residence (Villa de Álvarez), possibly because of the church connection through Francisco de Sales Vizcaíno, who served at the Parroquia de San Miguel del Espíritu Santo in Comala.

5. **Multiple dispensations** were required from the Church, suggesting the marriage was arranged carefully despite the bride's young age. The Church had to issue dispensations from banns, send letters to parishes in Guadalajara and Villa de Álvarez, and authorize a non-parishioner marriage.

## PART III: THE PLACES

### 3.1 Rancho de Los González, Tonaya, Jalisco (Birthplace)

Irene was born in **Rancho de Los González**, one of 29 inhabited localities within the municipality of Tonaya, in the Sierra de Amula region of southern Jalisco. This was a small, isolated farming settlement in rugged mountainous terrain.

**Tonaya** is a municipality with pre-Hispanic roots, founded by tribes of Toltec origin. Its economy centered on **mezcal production** from green agave (Agave angustifolia Haw), a tradition stretching back over a century, along with subsistence agriculture and forestry. The town's name derives from Nahuatl. Its church, **Nuestra Señora de la Asunción**, was built in the 1800s; its ruins, including a majestic bell tower, still stand.

The Sierra de Amula is a region of deep ravines, pine-oak forests at higher elevations, and semi-arid valleys. Elevations range from 680 to 2,860 meters. The town of Tonaya sits at approximately 824 meters above sea level. It was, and remains, a remote place.

For a girl born in 1924 in a rancho within this municipality, daily life meant subsistence farming, Catholic devotion, and the rhythms of an agricultural calendar. There was no electricity, no paved roads, no running water. Literacy was rare. The nearest cities were hours away by mule.

### 3.2 Comala, Colima (Place of Marriage)

Irene married Secundino in **Comala**, the cabecera (seat) of the municipality of Comala, in the state of Colima. The name derives from the Nahuatl word *comalli* (the flat cooking vessel) combined with the suffix *-an*, meaning "place of comals." The area was originally called Ajuchitlán, "Valley of the Flowers."

Comala sits at the border of Jalisco and Colima, making it a natural destination for families migrating south from the Sierra de Amula. It was historically an important coffee-producing region; the famous Hacienda de San Antonio, founded by German immigrant Arnold Vogel, produced coffee that was served at the Waldorf Astoria in New York.

The Parroquia de San Miguel del Espíritu Santo, where Irene and Secundino's religious marriage was celebrated, stands at V. Carranza No. 49 in Comala. The parish still maintains the original marriage register (Book 8, page 188, entry No. 926).

Comala is globally famous as the setting of **Juan Rulfo's novel *Pedro Páramo*** (1955), one of the foundational works of Latin American literature. The novel's opening line, "Vine a Comala porque me dijeron que acá vivía mi padre, un tal Pedro Páramo" ("I came to Comala because I was told that my father, a certain Pedro Páramo, lived there"), has made the town's name synonymous with memory, ghosts, and the search for a lost father. In 1961, the town painted all its facades white, earning the nickname "White Village of America." It was designated a Pueblo Mágico (Magical Town) in 2002.

### 3.3 Villa de Álvarez, Colima (Place of Life and Death)

Irene spent most of her adult life in **Villa de Álvarez**, the smallest but most densely populated municipality in the state of Colima. Founded on September 15, 1550, it sits adjacent to the northwest side of the state capital, Colima, forming a single urban conurbation.

Her final residence was at **Paul P. Harris No. 26, Colonia Vistahermosa, Colima**. She died at a hospital or clinic at Av. Lapislázuli No. 250, Fracc. El Haya, Villa de Álvarez, the address matching a medical facility in the area.

Villa de Álvarez represented a more stable, urban environment compared to the isolated ranchos and small towns of her childhood. The Parish of San Francisco de Asís (1903) and the Ex Hacienda del Carmen (18th century) are among its historic landmarks. For a woman who migrated from the mountains of Jalisco, Villa de Álvarez offered something her birthplace could not: permanence.

## PART IV: THE TIMES

### 4.1 Born into the Aftermath of Revolution (1924)

Irene was born on May 24, 1924, just four years after the end of the Mexican Revolution (1910-1920). A decade of revolutionary violence had shattered the old social order, destroyed haciendas, displaced millions, and left a legacy of political instability. President Álvaro Obregón (1920-1924) was succeeded by **Plutarco Elías Calles** (1924-1928), who would ignite the next great conflict.

### 4.2 The Cristero War: La Cristiada (1926-1929)

The defining event of Irene's early childhood was the **Cristero War**, a peasant uprising against the anticlerical laws enforced by President Calles. The **Calles Law of 1926** severely restricted the Catholic Church's role in public life. On **July 31, 1926**, the Catholic Church leadership suspended all public worship in Mexico, denying millions their sacraments.

**Irene was two years old when the churches closed.**

The rebellion was fiercest in western and central Mexico, precisely in the states of **Jalisco, Colima, Michoacán, Guanajuato, and Zacatecas**. Tonaya, in the heart of Jalisco's Sierra de Amula, was in the epicenter. By 1928, Cristero fighters effectively controlled large swaths of territory across these states. The conflict was devastating:

- Government forces destroyed suspected rebel-supporting villages, conducted mass executions, and implemented **"reconcentración"** (forced resettlement of rural populations)
- Churches were closed, priests executed or driven into hiding
- Between **90,000 and 200,000 people died**
- Families celebrated marriages at midnight in hidden rooms, baptisms in caves, and Masses in private homes with priests who risked death

For a child aged 2 to 5 in a rancho of the Sierra de Amula, the Cristero War was the burning of churches, the disappearance of priests, federal soldiers in the hills, gunfire in the ravines, and the forced displacement of entire communities.

### 4.3 The Second Cristiada (1934-1939)

Peace in 1929 was fragile. In the mid-1930s, President Lázaro Cárdenas imposed **socialist education** in public schools, which rural Catholics saw as an assault on their faith. A second uprising followed. The worst years were **1934 and 1935**: state governments again closed churches, and priests "practically vanished." In Jalisco, parish priests sometimes participated directly in resistance against government-appointed teachers.

**Irene's entire childhood and adolescence, from birth (1924) through her marriage (1937), was bracketed by two successive religious wars**, both of which struck her home region with particular force. She was a child of the Cristiada.

### 4.4 A Thirteen-Year-Old Bride (1937)

Irene married at **age 13**. The legal minimum for girls was 14. The marriage required special dispensations from both the civil and religious authorities. This was not unusual in the context of 1930s rural Mexico:

- **Child marriage was deeply entrenched** in rural society. Girls were considered ready for marriage at puberty.
- **Economic pressures** drove families to arrange marriages early: one fewer mouth to feed, family alliances cemented through matrimony.
- **Irene's mother was dead.** With Crescenciana gone, the family may have sought the perceived security of establishing Irene in a new household.
- **A decade of war** (the Cristero conflict and its aftermath) had disrupted civil registries, scattered priests, and created a culture of urgency around life's milestones. With death a constant presence, families did not wait.
- The **ranchero culture** of western Mexico placed immense importance on protecting a girl's "honor" through early marriage.

Child marriage across Mexico was not made illegal until **2014**, when the Federal Congress set the minimum marriage age at 18 for both sexes.

### 4.5 Illiteracy and the Condition of Rural Women

The genealogical narrative describes the "condición" of Irene's family: "sobrias, lean ni escribir" (austere, [who could] neither read nor write). Irene was illiterate. In 1930s Mexico, illiteracy rates in rural areas exceeded 70%. For women in remote ranchos, the figure was even higher. Schools, where they existed at all, were targets during the Cristero Wars: government teachers were attacked by Cristeros, and churches that had provided informal education were closed by the government.

Women's lives in the ranchero culture of western Mexico were shaped by **marianismo**, the ideal of the Virgin Mary as a model of feminine virtue: nurturing, family-centered, subordinate to male authority. Women's labor, both domestic and agricultural, was categorized as "complementary" and largely invisible. A girl like Irene, who could not read or write, whose mother had died, and who married at 13, would have stepped directly from childhood into the full weight of adult womanhood: managing a household, bearing children, and sustaining a family through the sheer force of her labor.

She did this 18 times over. She raised 18 children.

## PART V: THE BLOODLINE AND JUAN RULFO

### 5.1 The Vizcaíno Family and Mexico's Greatest Writer

The family tree contains a parenthetical notation of extraordinary significance. Next to the name of the earliest Vizcaíno ancestor, it reads: **"Lucas (Juan Rulfo)"**.

**Juan Rulfo's full name was Juan Nepomuceno Carlos Pérez Rulfo Vizcaíno.** His maternal surname was **Vizcaíno**. His mother was **María Vizcaíno Arias** (1887-1927).

María's father, Rulfo's maternal grandfather, was **Carlos Vizcaíno Vargas** (1846-1920), who was **born in La Piña, municipality of Tonaya, Jalisco**, the same municipality where Irene Vizcaíno Pelayo was born in 1924.

Carlos Vizcaíno Vargas's father was **José Lucas Vizcaíno Preciado**, born in San Gabriel on October 18, 1816. This **José Lucas Vizcaíno** is almost certainly the "Lucas" referenced in Irene's family tree, with "Juan Rulfo" in parentheses to identify the famous descendant connected to this ancestor.

### 5.3 The Vizcaíno Dynasty of Southern Jalisco

Carlos Vizcaíno Vargas (son of José Lucas), Rulfo's grandfather, became a great hacendado (large landowner) in Apulco, near Tuxcacuesco, Jalisco. He sponsored the construction of the Temple of the Virgin of Refuge in Apulco (1892-1910). The Vizcaíno family's sphere of influence spanned the exact region where Irene's family lived: **Tonaya, San Gabriel, Tuxcacuesco, Apulco, and Sayula**.

This is the same zone of southern Jalisco that Juan Rulfo immortalized in his fiction. His novel *Pedro Páramo* and his short story collection *El Llano en Llamas* draw their landscapes, characters, and emotional power from this very region. Rulfo's childhood memories centered on this "zone south of Sayula, including San Gabriel, Apulco, Tuxcacuesco, and Tonaya, where his maternal grandparents owned haciendas."

**Irene Vizcaíno Pelayo was born and raised in the landscape that Juan Rulfo turned into literature.** They were blood relatives. She lived the life that he wrote about.

### 5.4 Francisco de Sales Vizcaíno: The Priest in the Family

The family tree lists **Francisco de Sales** as a "sacerdote" (priest) among the children of the earlier Vizcaíno generation. This is the same Francisco de Sales Vizcaíno who:

1. Represented Irene's father at the civil marriage ceremony in 1937
2. Signed the church marriage certificate as officiating priest ("Pbro. Francisco de Sales Vizcaíno")

His dual role (family member and priest) is a vivid illustration of the intertwining of Church and family in rural Catholic Mexico. He was, in essence, a Vizcaíno who married a Vizcaíno to an Ahumada, binding the families together through both blood and sacrament.

## PART VI: THE PELAYO LINEAGE

### 6.1 Origins of the Surname

The surname **Pelayo** derives from the Latin "Pelagius," from the Greek *pelagos* meaning "sea." It is historically associated with **Don Pelayo (c. 685-737)**, the Visigothic nobleman who founded the Kingdom of Asturias and initiated the **Reconquista**, the Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from the Moors. His victory at the **Battle of Covadonga (718)** is considered the starting point of the 770-year Christian campaign to reclaim Spain.

The surname is thus tied to Spanish Catholic identity and the idea of resistance, a resonance that echoes through the Cristero context in which Irene's Pelayo ancestors lived and fought.

### 6.2 The Pelayo Family of Jalisco

In Mexico, the surname Pelayo is carried by approximately 17,763 individuals, with many tracing their origins to southern Jalisco. The family tree traces Irene's maternal Pelayo line back five generations:

1. **Miguel Pelayo**
2. **Ignacio Pelayo** (son of Miguel)
3. **Vicente Pelayo** (son of Ignacio)
4. **Hilario Pelayo** (son of Vicente)
5. **Crescenciana Pelayo (Naranjo)** (daughter of Hilario; Irene's mother)

Note the depth of the Pelayo surname: **both of Irene's parents carried it**. Her father Faustino's full name was "Faustino Vizcaíno Pelayo" (his mother was a Pelayo), and her mother was "Crescenciana Pelayo Naranjo." In the small, endogamous communities of the Sierra de Amula, the Pelayo and Vizcaíno families had intermarried across multiple generations.

## PART VIII: DOCUMENTARY THEMES AND OPEN QUESTIONS

### 8.1 Core Documentary Themes

1. **A Woman of the Century:** Irene lived from 1924 to 2019, spanning nearly the entire 20th century and into the 21st. She was born into a world without electricity, paved roads, or civil rights for women, and died in a modern hospital in an urban municipality. Her life is a lens through which to view Mexico's transformation.

2. **The Cristero Legacy:** Irene's childhood was defined by religious warfare. The Cristero War and its aftermath shaped the migration patterns, family structures, and spiritual identity of an entire generation. The priest who married her (Francisco de Sales Vizcaíno) was a living embodiment of the Church that survived.

3. **The Bloodline of Literature:** The connection to Juan Rulfo is not metaphorical. Irene shared ancestors with Mexico's most important novelist. She was born in the landscape he immortalized, married in the town he made famous, and lived the campesino life he wrote about. She was, in a real sense, a character from his world who kept living after the books were closed.

4. **Motherhood as Epic:** Eighteen children in 26 years, from age 14 (Ma. Griselda, 1938) to age 39 (Alicia, 1964). Nine children in the first 11 years alone. This is a story of endurance, of physical and emotional labor on an almost incomprehensible scale.

5. **Migration and Roots:** The journey from Rancho de Los González to Villa de Álvarez follows the great migration of Mexico's 20th century: from isolated rural communities to small cities, from subsistence to commerce, from the mountains to the valley.

6. **Illiteracy and Memory:** Irene could not read or write. Her story was carried in oral tradition, in the family trees drawn by hand, in the documents filed by officials and priests. Roberto Ahumada Vizcaíno's decision to write the genealogical narrative in 2020 was an act of preservation: committing to paper what had been carried in memory.

### 8.2 Open Questions for Further Research

1. **Exact birth date of Secundino Ahumada Fuentes:** The marriage certificate (May 1937) says he was 21, suggesting a birth year of ~1916. The genealogical narrative suggests May 22, 1917. His birth certificate would resolve this.

2. **Date of Secundino's death:** Irene was a widow at the time of her death (2019). When did Secundino die? What were the circumstances?

3. **Date of Crescenciana Pelayo Naranjo's death:** Irene's mother was already dead by May 1937. When did she die? What were the circumstances? How old was Irene when she lost her mother?

4. **The Rulfo connection in more detail:** Is there a specific genealogical chart that shows the exact branching point between Irene's Vizcaíno line and Rulfo's? How many generations separate them? Were they second cousins? Third? The family tree suggests the connection goes through Alejo Vizcaíno (Irene's line) and Carlos Vizcaíno Vargas (Rulfo's line), both children of José Lucas Vizcaíno and Clara Vargas. If so, Faustino Vizcaíno and Carlos Vizcaíno Vargas would have been brothers, making Irene and María Vizcaíno Arias (Rulfo's mother) first cousins, and Irene and Juan Rulfo first cousins once removed.

5. **The migration story:** What caused the family to leave Tonaya for Villa de Álvarez? Was it the Cristero War? Economic opportunity? Family connections? At what exact date did they arrive?

6. **Family oral histories:** Are there surviving children, grandchildren, or great-grandchildren who carry stories, memories, photographs, or objects from Irene's life?

7. **Roberto Ahumada Vizcaíno's knowledge:** As the author of the genealogical narrative (2020), Roberto is Irene's third child (born November 3, 1941). What additional details does he possess beyond what he wrote?

8. **Additional civil and church records:** Birth certificates, baptismal records, and death certificates for other family members would fill gaps. FamilySearch holds Catholic Church records for Colima (1707-1969) and Jalisco (1590-1995) that may contain relevant entries.

9. **Photographs:** The PDF's page 7 appears to contain a partial, washed-out image. Are there surviving photographs of Irene, Secundino, their children, or the family homes?
